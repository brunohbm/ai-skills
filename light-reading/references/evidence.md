# Confidence level of the recommendations

Consult this file whenever the user asks, “is this proven?”, when you need to justify a recommendation, or before citing any numbers.

## Strong foundation (broad consensus)

- Cognitive load and limited working memory. General model (Sweller and successors) that underpins the entire skill. Capacity is often estimated at about 4 units (Cowan); Miller’s old “7 ± 2” is considered optimistic.
- Known vocabulary helps comprehension. Strong and replicated correlation between vocabulary knowledge and reading comprehension. Frequent words are recognized faster (lexical frequency effect).
- Prior knowledge reduces effort. Schema theory and many comprehension studies: readers with more background understand and retain more of the same text. Practical implication: calibrate the level to the reader.
- Cohesion and clear references. Ambiguous anaphora and lack of connectives increase rereading. This is well established in discourse psychology.
- Multimedia: relevant images help, irrelevant ones hinder. Cognitive theory of multimedia learning (Mayer) and related principles (coherence, contiguity). Caveat: most studies focus on learning rather than everyday reading.
- Contrast and minimum font size. Based on readability research and accessibility guidelines (WCAG).

## Moderate foundation (plausible, with more indirect evidence)

- Letter spacing increases reading speed. There is a study by Zorzi et al. (2012) with children, with larger gains in dyslexic readers. Effects in typical adults are smaller and less established. Applying slightly open letter spacing is safe; promising “X% faster” is not.
- Short paragraphs, headings, and chunking. A strong usability recommendation (Nielsen Norman Group, web writing) with support in segmentation studies, though the magnitude varies by text type and task.
- Line width of 50–75 characters. A long-standing typographic convention; empirical evidence exists, but it is modest and mixed.
- Complex syntax costs effort. Structures with long dependencies (for example, nested relative clauses) are harder, which is well documented. The specific role of passive voice is less clear: it depends on context, and passive voice is not always worse. That is why this skill says “prefer active when the agent matters,” not “avoid passive.”
- Complex metaphors cost more. There is experimental work on metaphor processing, but conventional metaphors are processed almost like literal ones; the cost lies in new or extended ones.

## Research claims from the original source that were NOT incorporated

The research that originated this skill contained some points that should be treated with caution:

- “Syntax explains 5–30% of the variation in comprehension.” This is a very broad range and no source was identified; the true value depends on age, language, and measurement. Do not cite.
- “Increasing vocabulary reduces effort by ~10–20%.” No verifiable source. Do not cite.
- “Readers with strong prior knowledge understand up to 4x more.” This number has no clear source. The directional effect is real; the figure is not supported.
- “German effect” appears in the original text, probably as an error for “germane load.” Also, the division of load into intrinsic/extrinsic/germane is debated in the literature, and some authors do not treat germane load as a separate category.
- Inconsistency in the spacing study: the original text says “adolescents” in one place and “children” in another, and it applies the numbers (5–13%) to readers in general. Treat this as a population-specific result.
- Overlapping colors/extending reading resistance: weak and controversial evidence; not included.
- Sans-serif on screen, serif in print: convention, not a well-demonstrated rule. The skill recommends only a readable font.
- Name-and-year citations (for example Kaiser 2011, Curtis & Longo 2001, Gilbert 2012, Stagg 2021) were not verified. Do not reproduce them as references without checking.

## General limitation

Most studies are performed with students, in English, and in laboratory tasks. The result in a real text, in Portuguese, for a specific reader, may differ. Treat the rules as working hypotheses and adjust based on user feedback.
