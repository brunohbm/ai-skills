# Techniques and evidence

Consult this file when the user questions the method, asks for its rationale, or needs to choose between techniques. Each item covers what it is, the strength of evidence, why it works, and its limitations. Numbers are approximate reference points, not promises; effects vary by material, age, and measure.

## 1. Usefulness ranking

Based primarily on the review by Dunlosky et al. (2013), which evaluated 10 study techniques:

| Usefulness | Techniques |
| --- | --- |
| High | Retrieval practice (self-testing), distributed practice (spacing) |
| Moderate | Interleaving, elaborative interrogation, self-explanation |
| Low | Summarizing, highlighting, keyword mnemonics, imagery for text, rereading |

Implication: highlighting and rereading are common ways to spend study time inefficiently. Replacing them with self-testing for the same amount of time often yields more.

## 2. Active retrieval (testing effect)

- **Evidence**: strong. A classroom meta-analysis (Yang et al., 2021; hundreds of studies and tens of thousands of students) found an average effect of about g ≈ 0.5 in favor of testing over restudying alone.
- **Mechanism**: each attempt to retrieve information strengthens the memory trace and access cues, and reveals what the learner does not actually know.
- **Limits**: some initial knowledge is needed (test after an initial exposure). Without feedback, the benefit decreases when the learner answers incorrectly. Free response often outperforms recognition, but well-designed multiple-choice questions can also help.

## 3. Spacing

- **Evidence**: strong and long-standing (Ebbinghaus, 1885; Cepeda et al., 2006, where the large majority of comparisons favored distributed practice).
- **Mechanism**: some forgetting between sessions makes the next retrieval more effortful, and that effort supports consolidation.
- **Interval**: Cepeda et al. (2008) suggest that the optimal interval between study sessions is a fraction of the time until the test: roughly 10 to 20% for deadlines several weeks to months away, and a smaller proportion for much longer deadlines. Treat this as a rule of thumb, not an exact formula.
- **Limits**: spacing requires planning and can feel slower. For an exam only a few hours away, the benefit is smaller; cramming with self-testing still beats rereading.

## 4. Self-explanation and elaboration

- **Evidence**: moderate. Meta-analyses of self-explanation show an average positive effect on comprehension (around d ≈ 0.5 in reviews of the field).
- **Mechanism**: explaining *why* requires integrating new information with prior knowledge and exposes gaps in understanding.
- **Limits**: superficial explanations or repeating the text do not count. It works best when the learner has a basic foundation and receives feedback on the explanation.

## 5. Worked examples and scaffolding

- **Evidence**: strong for beginners in procedural domains (math, programming, physics). This is a central finding in cognitive load theory (Sweller and colleagues).
- **Mechanism**: solving problems from scratch can overload the working memory of learners who do not yet have relevant schemas; studying examples frees capacity to build those schemas.
- **Expertise reversal effect**: as learners gain expertise, the same support can become counterproductive. Gradually fade it: a complete example, then a partially completed one, then independent work.

## 6. Interleaving

- **Evidence**: moderate and material-dependent. A meta-analysis (Brunmair and Richter, 2019) found a positive average effect with substantial variation. Benefits were clearer in math and in learning to distinguish similar categories (such as painting styles or problem types), and weak or absent for other material.
- **Mechanism**: requires choosing the right strategy for each item, training discrimination, and increasing the spacing between repetitions of the same type.
- **Limits**: practice can feel worse (with more errors) than blocked practice. Do not interleave before the learner has an initial grasp of each type. Avoid it when the items do not compete with one another.

## 7. Pretesting

- **Evidence**: moderate. Attempting an answer before learning, even when incorrect, can improve later retention of the correct answer (work by Kornell, Richland, and others).
- **Limits**: the benefit depends on providing the correct answer afterward with feedback. Questions completely beyond the learner's reach cause frustration; prefer questions where a guess is plausible.

## 8. Visual resources

- **Evidence**: moderate and specific. Combining well-integrated words and images can help (Mayer's multimedia learning and Paivio's dual-coding theory). Animations have a small advantage over static images for dynamic processes (Berney and Bétrancourt, 2016 meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.23).
- **Mind maps and concept maps**: findings are positive but modest and heterogeneous. The classic study by Farrand et al. (2002) found about a 10% improvement in factual retention in a small sample (dozens of students). Concept maps tend to work better when learners **construct** them rather than only looking at a completed map.
- **Rule of thumb**: use the assistant's visual to provide an initial structure, then ask the user to recreate or complete it from memory. Avoid excessive color and decoration; irrelevant elements add load without helping.

## 9. Feedback

- **Evidence**: strong overall, with nuance. Feedback that explains why an answer is right or wrong is better than right/wrong feedback alone. The ideal timing (immediate or slightly delayed) varies by task.
- **Practice**: give prompt feedback for new concepts and procedures. For consolidation, it can also help to let the learner try to correct an answer before showing the solution.

## 10. Illusion of fluency

Rereading, seeing someone else's solution, or hearing a clear explanation can create the feeling of "I understand." People judge learning by how easy it feels in the moment, but that ease does not predict future performance. Bjork calls conditions that hinder immediate performance but improve retention (spacing, interleaving, testing) "desirable difficulties." Teach users to question the feeling that "this is easy."

## 11. Techniques with little or no evidence

- **Learning styles** (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) as a criterion for choosing a method: there is no convincing experimental support. Adapt the format to the *content* (maps for structure, practice for skills, audio for pronunciation), not to a person's "style."
- **Pomodoro and fixed lesson durations**: the rationale (stopping before fatigue and sustaining focus) is reasonable, but there is no strong evidence for exactly 25 minutes. Treat it as a practical convention.
- **Reading faster without losing comprehension**: speed-reading does not overcome the speed-comprehension trade-off.
- **"We use 10% of our brains"** and percentage-based learning pyramids (we remember 10% of what we read, 20% of what we hear, and so on): unsupported myths.

## 12. Learning with AI: risks

- **Outsourcing effort**: asking for a ready-made answer can produce good performance while assistance is available and worse performance when it is removed. In an experimental study with math students (Bastani et al., 2024), unrestricted access to an assistant improved performance on assisted tasks but worsened results on an unaided test. A tutor version that gave hints instead of answers reduced this harm. This is why the skill says not to solve what the user needs to practice.
- **Model errors**: fluent explanations can be wrong or oversimplified. Verify claims in proportion to the risk and consult primary sources for specific facts.
- **Meaningful personalization**: AI can generate tailored examples, questions, and feedback on demand. Use this advantage; a book cannot do it in the same way.

## 13. Common claims not to repeat as facts

Study advice often mixes robust findings with unsupported claims. Avoid:

- Tables that label each method's "learning speed: fast/slow" or "engagement: high/low" as if these were research measurements. They are almost always impressions, not results.
- Claims that "bullets of up to 10 words" or similar formatting rules are proven to improve learning. These may be writing conventions, but there is no direct evidence that they improve learning.
- Numbers from a single small study (such as the roughly 10% map result) presented as guaranteed effects.
- Any promise to "learn X in N hours" without defining what "learn" means.

## 14. Transfer

- **Evidence**: depends on the domain, task, and distance between contexts. Performance on one exercise does not guarantee application in another context.
- **Implication**: when the goal is application, include problems or contexts that do not superficially reproduce the studied example.
- **Limit**: solving the original exercise demonstrates learning on that exercise, not transfer by itself.

## 15. Expertise reversal

- **Evidence**: supported by research on cognitive load and prior knowledge, especially in procedural learning. Support that helps beginners can become redundant once a learner has relevant schemas.
- **Implication**: diagnose prior knowledge and gradually fade worked examples and scaffolding.
- **Limit**: do not remove support automatically; adjust it to observed performance.

## 16. Pretraining

- **Evidence**: a multimedia learning principle supported by research on prior knowledge and cognitive load; the effect depends on the material and what the learner already knows.
- **Implication**: before a complex explanation, introduce the names and functions of essential components when this helps the user follow their relationships.
- **Example**: in an introduction to Docker, identify image, container, registry, volume, and network before explaining how they interact.

## 17. Segmentation

- **Evidence**: segmentation studies support dividing some complex material into manageable parts, but the benefit depends on the content, pacing, and prior knowledge.
- **Implication**: split material when there are many new elements, a change in goal, or a conceptual dependency that deserves separate attention.
- **Limit**: do not impose a fixed duration or fragment the content so much that connections between parts disappear.

## 18. Signaling

- **Evidence**: signaling principles in multimedia learning suggest possible benefits from directing attention to relevant elements and relationships; results vary by material and design.
- **Implication**: identify the central idea, dependencies, and critical differences to guide attention, for example: "X depends on Y" or "the critical difference between A and B is Z."
- **Limit**: signaling should clarify structure, not add decorative emphasis or substitute for understanding.
